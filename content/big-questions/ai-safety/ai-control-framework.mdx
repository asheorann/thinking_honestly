After reading *The Case for Ensuring Powerful AIs Are Controlled*, some key takeaways:

## Core argument

Labs should make sure safety measures apply even if AIs are misaligned and try to subvert them.

Key distinction the field conflates: **alignment** (AI doesn't want to cause harm) vs **control** (AI can't succeed even if it does). Most frontier labs focus on alignment, not control.

AI control aims to mitigate risks from models intentionally trying to seize power, called **scheming**, nearly the same as what Hubinger calls deceptive alignment.

## The window

Won't work for arbitrarily powerful AIs, but should work for the first wave capable of reducing risk from subsequent AIs.

Depends on a window existing where AI is transformatively useful but not yet uncontrollable.

— Where are we in that window right now?

Goal: "if an AI is scheming, it won't successfully cause a catastrophe before we catch it"

Catching an AI red-handed counts as a win condition.

## Evaluation

Evaluating control just requires evaluating capabilities?

— Is the idea that you're only testing what the AI can/can't do, sidestepping whether it's deceiving you about its internals?

— How do you actually measure capability, and where is the line separating "capable enough to be untrusted" from "not"?

Contrast: evaluating alignment is hard because a scheming AI can just fake being aligned during tests.

— Has anything major changed since this was published?

## Security mindset

Managing scheming risk should mirror managing security risk:
1. Characterize plausibility of attacks across attacker types
2. Argue that even with the best available strategy, each attacker type is unlikely to succeed

Good analogy: serious security teams assume some employees are compromised and design robustness around that, rather than trying to guarantee no employee is ever compromised.

## Control evaluations

To evaluate control, run a **control evaluation**, treating all models above a capability threshold as untrusted.

Red team finds attack strategies, trains models to execute them, then estimates probability of an unacceptable outcome.

Key assumption: red team is at least as good as the model at finding attacks.

Scheming AIs are actually at a disadvantage:
1. Less serial thinking time
2. Less info about countermeasures
3. Can't iterate without getting caught

Control evaluations are relatively easy to agree on as a standard, good for policy.

**Meta-level adversarial evaluations**: broader category of red-teaming a protocol. Control evals are a specific version assuming persistent scheming, targeting the actual deployment protocol, black-box only. Sleeper agents paper is an example that is NOT a control eval.

## Control techniques

1. Expensive human oversight for subtle dangerous actions
2. Cheap automated monitoring to flag actions before execution
3. Limiting untrusted AI affordances and access
4. Security hardening

## Using safe AI to prevent unsafe AI

Interesting section on using the first safe powerful AI to prevent catastrophe from subsequent unsafe ones:
1. Alignment
2. Security
3. Defense/hardening
4. Detection
5. Enforcement
6. Strategic advising

## Honest limitation

Once AI is sufficiently powerful control breaks down. This is an argument for buying time not solving the problem permanently.
